<p>An airdrop is a distribution of tokens to wallet addresses, usually as a reward for doing something useful for a project before it launched. Test a network, provide liquidity, bridge assets, or complete community tasks — these are common actions projects use to spread awareness and reward early supporters.</p>

            <p>The appeal is obvious: you can end up with real tokens for nothing but time. The catch is that the same mechanic attracts an enormous number of scammers, and one careless click can expose a wallet or drain it completely.</p>

            <h2>Step 1 — Set up a dedicated airdrop wallet</h2>
            <p>Never hunt airdrops with the wallet that holds your savings. Create a completely separate wallet and treat it as disposable.</p>

            <p>This single habit is the most valuable thing in this article. If a drainer catches you, it drains the burner — not your portfolio. Fund it only with the small amount needed for gas and basic testing.</p>

            <ul>
              <li><strong>Use a software wallet</strong> you are comfortable with, or a separate hardware wallet if you are farming at scale.</li>
              <li><strong>Write the seed phrase on paper.</strong> Never photograph it, never type it into a notes app, never paste it into a chat.</li>
              <li><strong>Keep the burner separate from your main wallet.</strong> Do not import both into the same browser extension.</li>
            </ul>

            <h2>Step 2 — Verify the source before you touch anything</h2>
            <p>Almost every airdrop scam begins the same way: a link that looks right. The domain might be claim-projectxyz.com instead of projectxyz.com, or use a near-identical name that tricks you into thinking it is official.</p>

            <p>The rule: <strong>never reach an airdrop page through a link someone sent you.</strong> Not in Telegram, not in Discord DMs, not in a reply to a tweet you made. Go to the project's official website or verified social profile before connecting anything.</p>

            <div class="callout danger">
              <div class="callout-title">Never do this</div>
              <p>If anyone — including an account that looks like official support — asks for your seed phrase or private key to "verify" or "unlock" your airdrop, it is a scam, 100% of the time.</p>
            </div>

            <h2>Step 3 — Check eligibility properly</h2>
            <p>Most real airdrops provide a checker tool where you paste your public wallet address. Pasting a <em>public address</em> is safe — it's public information.</p>

            <p>What deserves caution is a "checker" that asks you to connect your wallet and sign a message before it shows you anything. A basic eligibility checker should not need your seed phrase, private key, or a suspicious wallet signature.</p>

            <p>If a checker asks you to connect, close the tab and find another route to the information.</p>

            <h2>Step 4 — Understand what you're signing</h2>
            <p>When you actually claim, you will be asked to sign a transaction. Before you approve it, check two things:</p>

            <ol>
              <li><strong>What function is being called.</strong> Your wallet usually shows a plain-language summary. "Claim" or "Approve spending cap" are normal. An unlimited token approval to an unknown contract is not.</li>
              <li><strong>Where the transaction is going.</strong> Confirm the contract address matches the one published on the project's official documentation.</li>
            </ol>

            <div class="callout warn">
              <div class="callout-title">Watch the approval amount</div>
              <p>Many claim flows require a token approval. If the wallet asks you to approve <em>unlimited</em> spending of a valuable token, and the project only needs a small amount, consider whether the request is legitimate.</p>
            </div>

            <h2>Step 5 — Claim, then clean up</h2>
            <p>Once the claim succeeds, don't just walk away. Spend five minutes closing the loop:</p>

            <ul>
              <li><strong>Revoke approvals</strong> you no longer need, using a reputable approval-revocation tool.</li>
              <li><strong>Move any valuable tokens</strong> out of the burner wallet to somewhere safer.</li>
              <li><strong>Disconnect the wallet</strong> from any site you were connected to. Connection alone is low-risk, but there's no reason to leave it open.</li>
            </ul>

            <h2>The 7 mistakes that cost people their tokens</h2>
            <ol>
              <li><strong>Using a main wallet.</strong> The single biggest cause of catastrophic losses.</li>
              <li><strong>Clicking links from DMs.</strong> Even from accounts that look verified.</li>
              <li><strong>Signing blind.</strong> Approving a transaction without reading what it does.</li>
              <li><strong>Believing "gas fee" requests.</strong> A real airdrop never asks you to send crypto to receive crypto.</li>
              <li><strong>Ignoring token approvals.</strong> Old approvals to sketchy contracts stay live for years.</li>
              <li><strong>Rushing a deadline.</strong> Urgency is a manipulation tactic. Fake "claim expires in 10 minutes" timers exist for exactly this reason.</li>
              <li><strong>Trusting a screenshot.</strong> Anyone can screenshot a fake balance.</li>
            </ol>

            <h2>The honest expectation</h2>
            <p>Most airdrop farming produces nothing. You'll spend hours on testnets that never launch a token, or qualify for a distribution that turns out to be worth a few dollars. That's the normal experience, not a sign of failure.</p>

            <p>Treat it as a low-cost lottery ticket: spend time, never spend more than you'd be comfortable losing on gas, and never let the possibility of a payout push you into a signature you do not understand.</p>
