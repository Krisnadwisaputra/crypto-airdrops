<p>Tap-to-earn games exploded in popularity because they removed every barrier: no wallet setup, no gas fees, no technical knowledge. You tap a screen, accumulate points, and hope those points convert into a real token.</p>

          <p>The model is clever. The economics are brutal. Here's how to tell the difference.</p>

          <h2>How the model works</h2>

          <ol>
            <li><strong>You tap or complete tasks</strong> to accumulate an in-game point balance.</li>
            <li><strong>The game grows</strong> as players invite friends to boost their earnings rate.</li>
            <li><strong>A token launches,</strong> and points convert at some ratio.</li>
            <li><strong>Players sell,</strong> and the token price does whatever supply and demand dictate.</li>
          </ol>

          <h2>The structural problem</h2>

          <p>Nearly everyone playing is there to earn, not to use the product. That means at launch, there is a huge amount of supply hitting the market and very little reason for anyone to hold.</p>

          <p>A common risk is heavy selling pressure after launch as participants become able to trade their rewards. Token prices can be highly volatile, and early price movements do not guarantee what happens next.</p>

          <div class="callout warn">
            <div class="callout-title">The math most players miss</div>
            <p>Your point balance is meaningless without knowing the conversion ratio and the total supply. A billion points split among fifty million players is not a large payout. Games rarely publish these numbers before launch — which is itself information.</p>
          </div>

          <h2>Signals of a game that might hold up</h2>

          <ul>
            <li><strong>There's a real product behind it.</strong> The game is a user-acquisition funnel for something that actually exists — an exchange, a wallet, a chain with real usage.</li>
            <li><strong>Tokenomics are published before launch.</strong> Conversion ratios, vesting, and total supply disclosed in advance.</li>
            <li><strong>Emission is limited.</strong> Diminishing returns over time rather than infinite point printing.</li>
            <li><strong>The team has a track record.</strong> Named developers, prior shipped work, an audited contract.</li>
            <li><strong>There's a sink for the token.</strong> A reason to hold or spend it inside the ecosystem, not just sell it.</li>
          </ul>

          <h2>Signals of a game that won't</h2>

          <ul>
            <li>Anonymity everywhere — no team, no entity, no contracts.</li>
            <li>"Coming soon" that keeps slipping.</li>
            <li>Aggressive monetization before launch (paid boosts, paid energy refills).</li>
            <li>Referral mechanics that dwarf actual gameplay.</li>
            <li>No published conversion ratio even after the token exists.</li>
          </ul>

          <h2>How to approach them sanely</h2>

          <ol>
            <li><strong>Never pay.</strong> If the game asks for real money to boost earnings, treat it as a purchase, not an investment — and assume it's gone.</li>
            <li><strong>Cap your time.</strong> A few minutes a day. Not hours. The expected value doesn't justify more.</li>
            <li><strong>Don't connect your main wallet.</strong> Use a burner if a wallet connection is required.</li>
            <li><strong>If you choose to sell, remember that prices can be highly volatile after launch. Do not assume early price movements will continue.</li>
            <li><strong>Expect nothing.</strong> Then any payout is a pleasant surprise.</li>
          </ol>

          <h2>The verdict</h2>

          <p>Tap-to-earn games are a low-cost lottery ticket with a mild entertainment component. As a hobby that might occasionally pay out, fine. As an income strategy, no — the tokenomics are stacked against late participants by design.</p>

          <p>If you enjoy the game, play it. Just don't confuse tapping with investing.</p>
