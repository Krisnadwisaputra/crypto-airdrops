Step-by-Step Guide to Claiming Legit Crypto Airdrops

Krisna Dwi Saputra 8 min read Reviewed for the 2026 season

An airdrop is a distribution of tokens to wallet addresses, usually as a reward for doing something useful for a project before it launched. Test a network, provide liquidity, bridge assets, hold a specific token, complete quests — the project decides what counts, then sends tokens to the wallets that qualify.

The appeal is obvious: you can end up with real tokens for nothing but time. The catch is that the same mechanic attracts an enormous number of scammers, and one careless click can empty a wallet. This guide walks through the whole process in the order you should actually do it.

Step 1 — Set up a dedicated "airdrop wallet"

Never hunt airdrops with the wallet that holds your savings. Create a completely separate wallet and treat it as disposable.

This single habit is the most valuable thing in this article. If a drainer catches you, it drains the burner — not your portfolio. Fund it only with the small amount needed for gas fees.

  • Use a software wallet you're comfortable with, or a separate hardware wallet if you're farming at scale.
  • Write the seed phrase on paper. Never photograph it, never type it into a notes app, never paste it into a chat.
  • Keep the burner separate from your main wallet. Do not import both into the same browser extension.

Step 2 — Verify the source before you touch anything

Almost every airdrop scam begins the same way: a link that looks right. The domain might be claim-projectxyz.com instead of projectxyz.com, or use a Cyrillic character that renders identically to a Latin one.

The rule: never reach an airdrop page through a link someone sent you. Not in Telegram, not in Discord DMs, not in a reply to a tweet you made. Go to the project's official channel yourself, from a bookmark you saved earlier, and follow the link from there.

Never do this

If anyone — including an account that looks like official support — asks for your seed phrase or private key to "verify" or "unlock" your airdrop, it is a scam, 100% of the time. No legitimate project needs it. Ever.

Step 3 — Check eligibility properly

Most real airdrops provide a checker tool where you paste your public wallet address. Pasting a public address is safe — it's public information.

What deserves caution is a "checker" that asks you to connect your wallet and sign a message before it shows you anything. A basic eligibility checker should not need your seed phrase or private key. If it asks you to sign a message, understand exactly what you are signing before continuing.

If a checker asks you to connect, close the tab and find another route to the information.

Step 4 — Understand what you're signing

When you actually claim, you will be asked to sign a transaction. Before you approve it, check two things:

  1. What function is being called. Your wallet usually shows a plain-language summary. "Claim" or "Approve spending cap" are normal. An unlimited token approval to an unknown contract is not.
  2. Where the transaction is going. Confirm the contract address matches the one published on the project's official documentation.
Watch the approval amount

Many claim flows require a token approval. If the wallet asks you to approve unlimited spending of a valuable token, and the project only needs a small amount, consider whether that risk is worth the airdrop. Approvals can be revoked later, but only if you remember to.

Step 5 — Claim, then clean up

Once the claim succeeds, don't just walk away. Spend five minutes closing the loop:

  • Revoke approvals you no longer need, using a reputable approval-revocation tool.
  • Move any valuable tokens out of the burner wallet to somewhere safer.
  • Disconnect the wallet from any site you were connected to. Connection alone is low-risk, but there's no reason to leave it open.

The 7 mistakes that cost people their tokens

  1. Using a main wallet. The single biggest cause of catastrophic losses.
  2. Clicking links from DMs. Even from accounts that look verified.
  3. Signing blind. Approving a transaction without reading what it does.
  4. Believing "gas fee" requests. A real airdrop never asks you to send crypto to receive crypto.
  5. Ignoring token approvals. Old approvals to sketchy contracts stay live for years.
  6. Rushing a deadline. Urgency is a manipulation tactic. Fake "claim expires in 10 minutes" timers exist for exactly this reason.
  7. Trusting a screenshot. Anyone can screenshot a fake balance.

The honest expectation

Most airdrop farming produces nothing. You'll spend hours on testnets that never launch a token, or qualify for a distribution that turns out to be worth a few dollars. That's the normal outcome, not a failure.

Treat it as a low-cost lottery ticket: spend time, never spend more than you'd be comfortable losing on gas, and never let the possibility of a payout push you into a signature you don't understand.
